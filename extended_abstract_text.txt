ABSTRACT 
Lightweight cement mortar produced with Expanded 
Polystyrene (EPS) is an effective approach for reducing the 
self-weight of cement-based elements; however, EPS 
inclusion commonly reduces strength and toughness due to 
poor interfacial bonding, high buoyancy, and weak stress 
transfer within the cement matrix. This study investigates 
the synergistic effects of Rice Husk Ash (RHA) and 
Polypropylene (PP) fiber on the performance of modified 
EPS cement mortar. Surface-coated EPS beads were used as 
lightweight aggregate, RHA was incorporated as a 
pozzolanic cement replacement, and PP fibers were added as 
micro-reinforcement to improve crack resistance. The mix 
proportions were developed using Response Surface 
Methodology (RSM) with a 
Face-Centered 
Central 
Composite Design (CCF), while the Absolute Volume 
Method was used for accurate batching of the lightweight 
and pozzolanic constituents. The performance of the mortar 
was evaluated through compressive strength, flexural 
strength, 
density, 
water 
absorption, 
toughness, 
and 
microstructural observations.  
The results showed that EPS replacement reduced mortar 
density but caused a reduction in mechanical strength due to 
its void-like behavior. The incorporation of RHA improved 
the cementitious matrix through pozzolanic reaction and 
pore refinement, with 10% RHA giving the most effective 
strength recovery. Surface coating of EPS significantly 
improved the interfacial transition zone by promoting 
mechanical interlocking between the EPS particles and 
cement paste. The addition of PP fibers further enhanced 
flexural 
performance 
and 
toughness 
by 
bridging 
microcracks and controlling brittle failure, with an optimum 
fiber dosage identified before the onset of fiber balling and 
strength reduction. Overall, the combined use of modified 
EPS, RHA, and PP fiber produced a lightweight cement 
mortar with improved mechanical performance, reduced 
water absorption tendency, and enhanced toughness. This 
study confirms that a properly optimized ternary mortar 
system can balance density reduction, strength development, 
and 
crack 
resistance 
for 
lightweight 
cement-based 
construction applications. 
Keywords— Modified EPS cement mortar, Rice Husk Ash, 
Polypropylene fiber, lightweight mortar, Response Surface 
Methodology, compressive strength, flexural strength, 
density, water absorption, toughness. 
 
 
 
I. INTRODUCTION  
The construction industry is increasingly shifting from 
traditional in-situ wet construction to factory-controlled off-
site prefabrication because it improves quality, reduces 
material waste, and shortens construction time [1]. In high-
density residential and commercial projects, prefabricated 
non-load-bearing wall panels are widely used as internal 
partitions and façade cladding. These panels are attractive 
because they provide good dimensional accuracy, smooth 
finished surfaces, and faster installation, which is important in 
urban areas facing labor shortages and rapid housing demand 
[2, 3]. 
However, conventional cement mortar and fine concrete have 
high density, generally around 2200–2400 kg/m³, which 
increases the dead weight of precast wall elements. Heavy 
panels require high-capacity transport and lifting equipment, 
increase handling stresses during demolding, stacking, and 
installation, and may cause early cracking or mechanical 
failure before the panel reaches full strength [4]. Therefore, 
developing lightweight mortar with a proper balance between 
strength, density, and toughness is essential for prefabricated 
wall applications [5]. 
Expanded Polystyrene (EPS) beads can reduce mortar density 
because of their very low weight and high air content, but raw 
EPS has a smooth, hydrophobic surface that weakens bonding 
with cement paste [2, 3]. Surface coating with polymer binder 
and fine M-sand improves mechanical interlocking and 
reduces segregation [3]. Rice Husk Ash (RHA) is used as a 
pozzolanic material that reacts with calcium hydroxide and 
forms additional C-S-H gel, improving the cement matrix 
around EPS particles [6]. Polypropylene (PP) fibers are added 
to bridge cracks, control shrinkage, and improve post-
cracking toughness [7]. Thus, the combined use of coated 
EPS, RHA, and PP fiber can produce a lightweight, stronger, 
and tougher cement mortar suitable for precast partition 
panels [8]. This ternary approach addresses density reduction, 
matrix refinement, and crack resistance in one optimized 
mortar system. While EPS lightweight mortars, RHA-
modified pastes, and PP-fiber-reinforced composites have 
each been studied individually, their combined optimization 
as a single ternary system — balancing density, strength, and 
toughness together through Response Surface Methodology 
— has not been reported, and is the specific contribution 
addressed here.

II. MATERIALS AND EXPERIMENTS PROCEDURE 
A. Materials and Apparatus 
 
The main materials used in this 
study were Ordinary Portland 
Cement, 
manufactured 
sand, 
coated 
Expanded 
Polystyrene 
beads, 
Rice 
Husk 
Ash, 
polypropylene 
fibers, 
and 
superplasticizer. 
Tokyo 
Super 
Ordinary Portland Cement of 
strength class 42.5 R, conforming 
to SLS 107:2015, was used as the 
binder. The cement had a specific gravity of 2.97, Blaine 
fineness of 3500 cm²/g, standard consistency of 30%, initial 
setting time of 160 min, and final setting time of 600 min. 
Its 2-day and 28-day compressive strengths were 25 N/mm² 
and 55 N/mm², respectively [9]. 
 
Manufactured sand obtained from 
crushed metamorphic rock was used as 
the fine aggregate. Its grading, specific 
gravity, water absorption, bulk density, 
and moisture content were evaluated 
using standard ASTM procedures. 
Expanded Polystyrene beads were 
used as lightweight aggregate to 
reduce mortar density. Since raw EPS 
has poor bonding with cement paste, 
the beads were coated using Polyvinyl 
Acetate (PVAc) binder and fine M-sand to improve surface 
roughness and mechanical interlocking [10]. 
 
Rice Husk Ash was used as a supplementary cementitious 
material. It was sieved through 600 µm, ball milled at about 
96 rpm, and its final fineness was checked using a 53 µm 
wet sieve. Polypropylene fibers of 6 mm length, 25 µm 
diameter, 0.91 g/cm³ density, and 560 MPa tensile strength 
were added to improve crack resistance and toughness. 
FAIRFLO S superplasticizer, a modified sulphonated 
naphthalene polymer, was used at 0.6–1.5% by cement 
weight to maintain mortar workability at 110 ± 5 mm flow. 
 
The main apparatus included a laboratory ball mill, 
mechanical pan mixer, standard 40 × 40 × 160 mm steel 
prism moulds, vibrating table, pycnometers, graduated 
cylinders, sieve shaker, drying oven, muffle furnace, mortar 
flow table, Universal Testing Machine, and SEM–EDS 
equipment for microstructural analysis 
 
B. Experimental Procedure 
a. Material Characterization and Processing 
 
All raw materials were characterized before mix preparation 
to confirm their suitability. OPC specific gravity was 
determined using kerosene pycnometer. M-sand was tested 
for particle size distribution according to ASTM C136[11], 
and its grading was checked with BS 882[12] Zone M 
requirements. Specific gravity, 
water absorption, bulk density, 
and moisture content were 
also measured using standard 
ASTM methods. RHA was 
evaluated 
for 
chemical 
composition using SEM–EDS, 
loss on ignition at 750°C for 2 
hours, and fineness using a 53 
µm wet sieve. EPS beads 
were tested using a hydrostatic water displacement method 
due to their very low density.  
 
 
RHA was dry-sieved through 600 µm mesh and ball milled 
at about 96 rpm for 2 hours. For EPS coating, 100 g of raw 
EPS was mixed with 250 g of PVAc emulsion for 5 minutes, 
followed by fine M-sand addition and another 5 minutes of 
mixing. The coated EPS beads were dried for 24 hours 
before use. 
 
b. Mix Design and Mortar Preparation 
Mix proportions were developed using Response Surface 
Methodology (RSM) with a Central Composite Design 
(CCD). The Absolute Volume Method was used to ensure 
that all constituents, water, and air produced 1.0 m³ of mortar. 
In Phase 1, the water-to-binder and sand-to-binder ratios were 
varied to identify the optimum plain mortar matrix. In later 
phases, the baseline values were fixed at w/b = 0.425 and s/b 
= 2.25, with 11 mix variations in each CCD phase. 
 
Mortar was mixed in a mechanical pan mixer. OPC and RHA 
were mixed dry for 2 minutes, followed by M-sand for 
another 2 minutes. About 80% of the water with 
superplasticizer was then added and mixed for 1.5 minutes. 
Coated EPS was added with the remaining 20% water and 
mixed for 3 minutes. For fiber mixes, 6 mm PP fibers were 
added gradually over 2 minutes, followed by a final 2-minute 
mixing. The flow was maintained at 110 ± 5 mm. 
 
c. Casting, Curing, and Testing 
 
Fresh mortar was cast into 40 × 40 
× 160 mm steel prism moulds in 
layers and compacted on a vibrating 
table at 50 Hz for 30 seconds per 
layer. Specimens were covered for 
12–24 hours, demolded, and water-
cured until 28 days. 
 
At 28 days, flexural strength was 
tested using a three-point bending setup with a 100 mm span 
and 50 ± 10 N/s loading rate. The broken halves were then 
tested for compressive strength using a 40 × 40 mm loading 
area at 2400 ± 200 N/s. Water absorption and apparent 
porosity were determined by oven drying at 105 ± 5°C for 24 
hours, 
followed 
by 
water 
immersion 
and 
mass 
measurements. 
 
d. Microstructural Analysis 
 
Selected fractured samples containing the aggregate–paste 
interface were prepared for SEM–EDS analysis. Hydration 
was arrested using isopropyl alcohol, and the samples were 
dried, epoxy mounted, polished, carbon taped, and gold 
coated. SEM images and EDS line scans were used to 
examine the interfacial transition zone and calcium-to-silicon 
ratio near coated EPS particles.

IV. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION  
 
The experimental results were discussed phase by phase to 
show the contribution of each modification: base matrix 
optimization, density reduction using coated EPS, RHA-
based pozzolanic densification, EPS coating validation, and 
PP fiber crack-bridging. Strength values were interpreted 
using the standard mortar prism strength procedure, while 
water absorption and porosity were used to judge the internal 
quality of the hardened matrix.

B. Base Mortar Optimization (Phase 1) 
 
 
Phase 1 established the reference mortar before adding RHA, 
EPS or PP fiber. The lowest water-to-binder ratio produced 
the highest strength, but it also made the mix too stiff for later

instead of the maximum-strength mix. This mix used w/b = 
0.425 and s/b = 2.25, giving an average compressive strength 
of 35.58 MPa, flexural strength of 4.75 MPa, 28-day density

This result agrees with absolute-volume mix proportioning

balanced [13]. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
C. Lightweight Aggregate and RHA Optimization (Phase 2) 
 
Phase 2 introduced coated EPS as the lightweight aggregate 
and RHA as the pozzolanic binder replacement. Increasing 
EPS clearly reduced density, but high EPS content caused a 
major reduction in compressive strength and toughness. The 
40% EPS mixes reached densities near 1719-1748 kg/m3, 
but their compressive strength dropped to about 4-5 MPa. 
This confirms that EPS acts as a low-stiffness inclusion and 
weakens the continuous cementitious skeleton when used at 
high volume. 
 
The best compromise was obtained using 10% RHA and 
20% coated EPS. This condition reduced density to about 
1993 kg/m3 while retaining about 16.78 MPa compressive 
strength and 3.26 MPa flexural strength. The apparent 
porosity remained close to the dense control, showing that 
the RHA helped compensate for the porosity introduced by 
EPS. This behaviour is consistent with RHA literature, 
where reactive silica improves strength and permeability by 
secondary C-S-H formation.

D. Microstructural and Chemical Interfacial Transition Zone 
(ITZ) Diagnostics (Phase 3) 
 
Phase 3 checked whether the strength improvement from 
RHA was supported by a chemical change at the interfacial 
transition zone (ITZ). The comparison used 40% EPS mixes 
with and without 20% RHA. The average Ca/Si ratio reduced 
from 24.85 to 5.65 after RHA addition, which indicates that 
the EPS boundary became less calcium-rich and more silica-
rich. This supports the pozzolanic interpretation that RHA 
consumed calcium hydroxide and supported secondary C-S-
H formation

stronger, increasing compressive strength from 4.20 MPa to 
7.15 MPa. These results show that the coating reduced EPS-

F. PP Fiber Toughness Optimization (Phase 5) 
 
Phase 5 introduced PP micro-fibers into the optimized 
coated-EPS matrix. The fiber mainly improved flexural 
response by bridging cracks and delaying brittle fracture. At 
0.5% fiber, flexural strength rose from 3.26 MPa to 3.59 
MPa and toughness increased from 14.71 to 18.24 mJ/mm³, 
while compressive strength eased slightly from 16.78 MPa 
to 15.62 MPa. Flexural strength continued rising to a 4.00 
MPa peak at 1.0% fiber, then fell back to 3.65 MPa at 1.5% 
and 2.89 MPa at 2.0%, as higher dosages caused fiber 
balling that trapped voids and drove compressive strength 
down further, to 11.85 MPa and 11.06 MPa respectively. 
Therefore, 0.5% PP fiber was selected as the practical 
optimum, balancing toughness gains against strength 
retention.

G. Overall Discussion 
 
The results confirm that the modified mortar performance 
was controlled by a balance between three mechanisms. 
Coated EPS reduced density, but excessive EPS weakened 
the continuous load-carrying skeleton. RHA improved the 
paste and ITZ by pozzolanic reaction, but it could not fully 
recover the strength loss caused by very high EPS volume. 
PP fiber improved flexural resistance and crack control, but 
only up to the dosage where dispersion remained good. 
Therefore, the most suitable mix was not the one with 
maximum EPS, maximum RHA or maximum fiber, but the 
balanced ternary combination. 
 
Based on the complete phase-wise evaluation, the 
recommended final combination is w/b = 0.425, s/b = 2.25, 
10% RHA, 20% PVAc/M-sand coated EPS and 0.5% PP 
fiber. This mix reduced density compared with the plain 
mortar, retained useful strength for lightweight precast 
applications, 
improved 
ITZ 
quality 
through 
RHA, 
strengthened EPS bonding through coating and improved 
post-cracking behavior through PP fiber bridging. 
 
V. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK  
 
This study successfully developed a lightweight modified 
EPS cement mortar by combining rice husk ash, surface-
coated expanded polystyrene beads, and polypropylene 
micro-fibers. The findings confirm that these three materials 
work as a complementary system rather than separate 
additives. RHA improved the cement matrix through 
pozzolanic reaction and pore refinement, coated EPS 
reduced density while improving aggregate–paste bonding, 
and PP fibers enhanced crack-bridging and post-cracking 
toughness. The optimized ternary mix containing 10% RHA, 
20% coated EPS, and 0.5% PP fiber achieved 15.62 MPa 
compressive strength, 3.59 MPa flexural strength, 18.24 
mJ/mm³ toughness, 5.79% apparent porosity, and 1993 
kg/m³ density, while reducing self-weight by nearly 300 
kg/m³ compared with conventional dense mortar. Therefore, 
the developed mortar can be considered suitable for 
sustainable 
non-load-bearing 
precast 
partitioning 
applications. 
 
Future work should focus on validating the long-term 
durability and practical performance of the optimized mortar 
under real service conditions. Tests such as chloride 
penetration, sorptivity, sulfate resistance, drying shrinkage, 
and weathering resistance should be carried out. Further 
studies should also evaluate thermal conductivity and 
acoustic performance, since EPS can improve insulation 
properties. Finally, the optimized mix should be scaled up 
from laboratory prisms to full-size precast wall panels and 
tested 
under 
handling, 
transportation, 
impact, 
and 
installation loads to confirm its suitability for industrial 
production.
