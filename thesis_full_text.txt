ABSTRACT 
Lightweight mortars for precast construction face an inherent engineering trade-off: 
reducing density severely compromises fracture resistance and structural capacity. 
This study demonstrates that the synergistic combination of three complementary 
materials rice husk ash (RHA) as a pozzolanic cement replacement, surface-coated 
expanded polystyrene (EPS) beads as lightweight aggregate, and polypropylene (PP) 
micro-fibers for crack control can simultaneously address this trade-off. Using 
Response Surface Methodology (RSM) based on a Face - Centered Central Composite 
Design (CCF), the mix proportions were systematically optimized while isolating three 
distinct microstructural mechanisms. First, chemical refinement via ball-milled RHA 
reduces capillary porosity and strengthens the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) through 
a secondary pozzolanic reaction, lowering the elemental Ca/Si ratio by over 77% to 
produce dense C-S-H gel. Second, the PVAc/M-sand physical surface coating on EPS 
beads neutralizes aggregate buoyancy and enforces mechanical interlocking, 
recovering up to 70% of lost compressive capacity at high volume fractions. Finally, 
PP micro-fiber bridging recovers the fracture resistance and handling toughness lost 
through lightweight aggregate incorporation. 
Water absorption and porosity measurements indicate targeted capillary pore 
refinement,

(10% RHA, 20% coated EPS, and 0.5% PP fiber) yielded a compressive strength of 
15.62 MPa, a flexural strength of 3.59 MPa, a handling toughness of 18.24 mJ/mm³, 
and an apparent porosity of 5.79%, while successfully shedding nearly 300 kg/m³ to 
achieve a bulk density of 1993 kg/m³. 
Ultimately, this study demonstrates that the synergistic integration of these three 
mechanisms produces superior performance outcomes compared to individual binary 
additions in isolation. The results validate that capillary porosity refinement (via 
pozzolanic reaction) and controlled toughness recovery (via fiber reinforcement) can 
safely offset the strength penalty inherent to lightweight aggregate substitution,

1. INTRODUCTION 
1.1.Background 
1.1.1. The Evolution of Prefabrication in Civil Infrastructure 
The contemporary construction industry is experiencing a structural paradigm shift, 
moving away from traditional, labor-intensive, in-situ wet concrete operations and 
toward industrialized off-site prefabrication [1]. In-situ masonry assembly is 
frequently hindered by inconsistent execution quality, substantial material waste, and 
extended construction schedules that rely heavily on ambient environmental curing 
conditions [2]. To bypass these systemic inefficiencies in the field, developers of civil 
infrastructure are increasingly turning to off-site construction (OSC) and automated, 
factory-controlled precast technologies [3]. 
A significant area of expansion within this industrialized framework is the mass 
production of non-load-bearing partitioning wall panels, which have largely replaced 
traditional brickwork and blockwork in high-density residential and commercial high-
rise projects [1]. In developing Asian economies like Sri Lanka, the growth of urban 
populations has sparked a sharp increase in the demand for vertical high-rises and 
apartments [4]. To meet these demands, local developers are adopting prefabricated 
wall elements such as internal separation barriers and facade cladding sheets. That 
offer exceptional dimensional accuracy and pre-finished surface qualities [5]. Because 
these panels arrive at the construction site ready for immediate mechanical anchoring, 
they eliminate the need for costly and time-consuming on-site wet plastering 
operations [1]. This accelerated installation cycle significantly shortens the structural 
critical path, lowers overall project overhead, and helps overcome the severe skilled-
labor shortages currently affecting urban development hubs [3, 4]. 
1.1.2. The Dead-Weight Penalty and Logistics Constraints 
Despite the evident productivity benefits of off-site prefabrication, traditional precast 
elements carry significant material and logistical drawbacks due to their high self-
weight [5]. Standard cement mortars and fine concretes have a dense, unyielding bulk 

<<< PAGE 19 >>>
 
2 
 
density that typically ranges between 2200 kg/m3and 2400 kg/m3. When cast into 
large-format architectural wall panels, this massive weight creates a severe dead-
weight penalty that burdens the entire logistics chain, necessitating specialized, high-
capacity transport fleets and heavy-duty site cranes just for basic handling and 
installation [6]. 
From a fundamental materials perspective, this excessive self-weight introduces major 
handling stresses. During early-age manufacturing cycles, specifically the first 12 to 
24 hours involving demolding, stripping, factory transit, and flat-bed stacking-the 
panel is highly susceptible to premature mechanical failure [7]. Because the material 
has not yet developed its full mature tensile capacity, handling forces induce localized 
bending moments and internal stress concentrations [8]. This often leads to the 
propagation of early-age plastic shrinkage, micro-cracking at the thin boundaries of 
the panel, or even catastrophic structural failure before the unit reaches the site [7]. 
Consequently, optimizing the performance of modern partitioning panels requires a 
careful balance of weight and strength, evaluated through the strength-to-unit density 
ratio [1]. This challenge in materials engineering is a primary driver for developing 
innovative, lightweight structural alternatives that minimize transportation costs and 
handling damage without compromising early-age structural integrity [5, 6]. 
1.1.3. Multi-Component Engineering Solutions 
To systematically address the logistical and mechanical handling challenges associated 
with heavy precast wall units, materials engineers have prioritized the development of 
engineered lightweight cementitious composites [2, 3]. Achieving a reliable reduction 
in bulk density involves substituting traditional dense fine aggregates with ultra-
lightweight particulate inclusions, such as Expanded Polystyrene (EPS) beads, which 
contain up to 98% air by volume within their closed-loop cellular structures [4]. 
However, raw EPS is notoriously smooth, chemically inert, and hydrophobic, leading 
to high fresh-state buoyancy and a structurally weak, detached Interfacial Transition 
Zone (ITZ) [4, 5]. To overcome these inherent flaws in geometric packing and 
bonding, this study utilizes Coated EPS beads [5]. Pre-treating the EPS beads with a 
water-insoluble polymer binder film encrusted with manufactured sand (M-sand) 

<<< PAGE 20 >>>
 
3 
 
artificially roughens the surface of the aggregate [5]. This engineered coating acts as a 
physical anchoring shell, preventing aggregate flotation during mixing and enforcing 
active mechanical interlocking with the surrounding cement matrix [5]. 
While the physical coating secures the aggregate-to-paste boundary, addressing the 
remaining deficits in matrix strength demands an integrated chemical and mechanical 
approach [5, 10]. Incorporating industrial and agricultural waste by-products, such as 
processed Rice Husk Ash (RHA), supplies a highly reactive source of amorphous 
micro-silica [6]. This fine ash initiates a secondary pozzolanic reaction that chemically 
consumes weak calcium hydroxide crystals and precipitates stable secondary calcium-
silicate-hydrate (C-S-H) gels, effectively densifying the cement paste immediately 
surrounding the pre-coated EPS boundaries [7]. Finally, because maximizing 
aggregate interlocking and densifying the matrix paste frequently results in a highly 
rigid and brittle material prone to abrupt failure under sudden handling stresses [8], 
short polymer micro-fibers, like Polypropylene (PP), are introduced to form a three-
dimensional reinforcing web [9]. These monofilament fibers serve as structural micro-
bridges across advancing crack fronts, which helps control early-age shrinkage and 
significantly enhances the composite's post-cracking energy absorption and toughness 
[9, 10]. By integrating these distinct mechanisms, the resulting ternary mortar 
capitalizes on physical coating roughening, chemical pozzolanic healing, and 
mechanical fiber bridging to strike an optimal balance of low density, high strength, 
and improved handling toughness ideally suited for industrial prefabrication [10]. 
1.2.Problem Statement 
1.2.1. The Interfacial Transition Zone (ITZ) Deficit of Raw EPS 
The primary obstacle preventing the direct volume substitution of dense aggregates 
with Expanded Polystyrene (EPS) beads in precast concrete elements is the 
microstructural and chemical incompatibility at the aggregate-paste boundary. Raw 
EPS possesses an exceptionally smooth, non-reactive, and strongly hydrophobic 
polymer surface energy profile [6]. When introduced into a fresh, highly alkaline, and 
hydrophilic Portland cement paste, this hydrophobicity repels the free mixing water 

<<< PAGE 21 >>>
 
4 
 
and initiates a micro-fluidic phenomenon known as the "wall effect" [11]. This 
geometric packing constraint prevents cement grains from closely adhering to the 
aggregate perimeter, creating localized, water-rich boundary films. As hydration 
progresses, these localized fluid pockets facilitate the preferential precipitation of 
large, plate-like calcium hydroxide (CH) crystals oriented parallel to the EPS bead 
surface, rather than generating dense calcium-silicate-hydrate (C-S-H) gel [11]. 
Consequently, the hardened composite develops a highly porous, detached, and 
structurally compromised Interfacial Transition Zone (ITZ) that is substantially 
weaker than the surrounding bulk cement paste. Because raw EPS beads possess 
negligible shear stiffness and zero chemical adhesion to the matrix, they behave 
mechanically as microstructural air voids or stress-concentrating defects [7]. Under 
external compressive or tensile loading paths, micro-cracks preferentially nucleate 
within this porous aggregate interface and propagate rapidly across the matrix cleavage 
planes [9, 11]. This microstructural deficit leads to poor mechanical properties and 
brittle macroscopic shearing even under low stress levels, directly restricting the use 
of untreated EPS in precast structural elements that must withstand lifting and 
installation loads. 
1.2.2. Workability-Toughness Trade-off 
Traditional binary material adjustments systematically fail to resolve the lightweight 
mortar paradox, as correcting a single performance liability often degrades another 
critical property. If researchers attempt to counteract the severe strength loss caused 
by EPS inclusions by introducing ultra-fine, porous agricultural Rice Husk Ash 
(RHA), the high specific surface area and cellular morphology of the ash particles 
drastically increase the physical water demand of the binder matrix [12]. This intense 
absorption depletes the free water required for fluid lubrication, leading to extreme 
slump loss, a rapid reduction in workability, and severe compaction difficulties during 
factory panel casting [12]. 
Conversely, attempting to remedy the matrix's low tensile capacity by introducing 
high-aspect-ratio Polypropylene (PP) micro-fibers presents a major rheological 
liability. When mixed into a fresh cementitious matrix, these fibers tend to ball, twist,

5 
 
and form dense structural clusters that trap macro-air voids within the paste [13]. 
Without systematic, multi-phase volumetric balancing, these binary interventions 
produce a critical trade-off: yielding either an unworkable fresh paste that cannot 
uniformly fill precast panel molds, or a hyper-brittle hardened composite susceptible 
to sudden cracking and failure under routine lifting, demolding, and transit stresses. 
1.2.3. The Empirical Validation Vacuum in the Sri Lankan Ecosystem 
The development of sustainable, lightweight prefabricated infrastructure is further 
bottlenecked by a regional engineering limitation: the empirical validation vacuum 
within the contemporary Sri Lankan construction ecosystem. Driven by rapid urban 
population growth and high-rise apartment construction across major urban sectors 
like Colombo, local developers are increasingly utilizing imported precast 
technologies, such as extruded lightweight concrete sandwich partition panels to 
bypass rising site labor costs and material delivery delays [4]. However, a critical 
review of regional materials literature reveals a distinct absence of standardized, 
published engineering datasets or predictive design models for multi-phase ternary 
composites utilizing localized, climate-specific raw materials and waste streams. 
Industrial precast operators attempting to produce lightweight building elements 
within Sri Lanka rely almost exclusively on trial-and-error recipe adjustments. This 
lack of structural optimization is highly inefficient when processing local agricultural 
by-products like Rice Husk Ash. Sri Lanka generates immense volumes of raw rice 
husk during seasonal milling; while a portion is used for factory boiler fuel, substantial 
quantities are routinely open-burned or field-dumped, producing a low-value 
byproduct with inconsistent amorphous silica quality and high carbon content [14, 15]. 
Currently, no localized standards exist to define the precise processing limits-such as

Furthermore, non-load-bearing partitioning panels must withstand vertical stacking

2. LITERATURE REVIEW 
2.1.Historical Evolution and Functional Boundaries of Binders 
The history of masonry construction is fundamentally linked to the development of 
binding materials designed to consolidate loose structural units into cohesive, load-
bearing configurations. In the earliest epochs of human civilization, dating back to the 
Neolithic period and the pristine developments of Mesopotamia and Babylonia, raw 
earth-primarily mud, silt, and alluvial clay, served as the primary ancestral framework 
for structural binders. From a fundamental engineering perspective, the operational 
function of mud mortar was not governed by chemical adhesion, but rather by 
topographical equalization and interfacial friction enhancement [16]. Raw masonry 
units, such as rubble or sun-dried adobe bricks, possessed highly irregular surface 
profiles. Stacking these rigid units directly on top of one another created severe stress 
concentrations at localized macro-roughness contact points, triggering premature 
tensile splitting and stone fracturing under vertical dead loads. Fresh mud mortar acted 
as a highly plastic, workable bedding cushion that flowed into these topographical 
surface irregularities. Upon physical drying, it successfully redistributed gravity loads 
uniformly across the entire nominal surface area of the masonry unit. However, clay-
based mud mortars lacked absolute hydraulic stability. The binding mechanism was 
purely physical and fully reversible; subsequent exposure to environmental moisture 
caused the clay mineral lattices to rehydrate, swell, lose internal cohesion, and undergo 
catastrophic structural wash-out. To combat severe drying shrinkage cracks and 
enhance bulk volumetric stability, ancient builders introduced organic and inorganic 
reinforcing fibers such as straw, reeds, and coarse sand aggregate marking the earliest 
historical deployment of multi-component particulate composite design [17]. 
As architectural scales transitioned toward permanent, monumental engineering 
structures, the water solubility and low shear strength of mud demanded a chemical 
paradigm shift, leading to the discovery of thermally calcined binders [18]. This 
calcined revolution utilized gypsum (𝐶𝑎𝑆𝑂4 ⋅2𝐻2𝑂) and limestone (𝐶𝑎𝐶𝑂3), 
requiring the application of deliberate thermal energy to transform stable raw minerals 
into chemically reactive, non-hydraulic binding powders [19]. In ancient Egypt, the 

<<< PAGE 27 >>>

thermal processing of gypsum rocks produced a rapidly setting plaster mortar, used 
predominantly as a structural lubricant to precision-align multi-ton stone blocks within 
monumental pyramid geometry. Concurrently, the thermal calcination of calcium 
carbonate emerged as a major technological advancement across Mediterranean 
civilizations. The manufacturing process relied on heating limestone rocks within 
primitive kilns up to approximately 900°C to induce thermal decomposition, releasing 
carbon dioxide gas and yielding highly reactive quicklime (𝐶𝑎𝑂):

The resulting quicklime was subsequently slaked with water in controlled pits to

𝐶𝑎𝑂+ 𝐻2𝑂→𝐶𝑎(𝑂𝐻)2 
When blended with sand aggregates, this material created traditional non-hydraulic 
lime mortar [18]. Unlike modern hydraulic cements, the mechanical strength

While non-hydraulic lime mortars offered superior durability over mud and 
accommodated minor structural foundation movements through high autogenous 
flexibility, their structural evolution was limited by two primary factors: they required 
direct atmospheric exposure to cure, and they possessed zero strength development or 
volumetric stability when submerged underwater. 
The true turning point in the chemistry of structural binders occurred in the 
Mediterranean basin during the 2nd century BCE, orchestrated by Roman builders who 
triggered the hydraulic pozzolanic revolution. The Romans discovered that blending 
conventional slaked lime paste with finely ground volcanic ash mined from the slopes 
of Mt. Vesuvius near Pozzuoli (termed pulvis puteolanus) generated a binding matrix 
that hardened independently of atmospheric exposure and developed immense 
mechanical strength underwater. This discovery established the foundational

chemistry of pozzolanic materials. Volcanic ash is rich in highly amorphous, reactive 
aluminosilicates (𝑆𝑖𝑂2 and 𝐴𝑙2𝑂3). When introduced into the highly alkaline liquid 
phase of a slaked lime paste (𝑝𝐻> 12.5), these disordered glassy silica networks 
dissolve and chemically react with the available 𝐶𝑎(𝑂𝐻)2ions. This reaction 
precipitates a dense, interlocking matrix of crystalline Calcium-Silicate-Hydrate (C-S-
H) and Calcium-Aluminate-Hydrate (C-A-H) mineral networks [20]. Unlike the weak 
physical drying of mud or the slow atmospheric carbonation of lime, the Roman 
pozzolanic reaction was a hydraulic chemical synthesis. The resulting cohesive C-S-
H gel network grew across the internal micro-voids between aggregate particles, 
permanently welding the fine aggregate matrix into a dense, stone-like composite. This 
technology allowed for the structural execution of massive, unreinforced masonry 
domes (such as the Pantheon) and marine harbor infrastructure that remains 
structurally intact over two millennia later [21]. 
Following a prolonged period of technological regression throughout the medieval era, 
systematic industrialization in the 19th century culminated in the invention and global 
dominance of Portland cement, establishing the modern binder paradigm. Patented by 
Joseph Aspdin in 1824, Portland cement was formulated by precisely proportioning a 
synthetic mixture of finely pulverized limestone and natural aluminous clay, burning 
the combination in high-temperature rotary kilns operating up to approximately 
1450°C [19]. This intensive thermal processing drives partial sintering, converting the 
raw minerals into dense clinker nodules rich in highly stable calcium silicate phases 
predominantly Alite (𝐶3𝑆) and Belite (𝐶2𝑆) [18]. When Portland cement is combined 
with water, these mineral phases undergo rapid, exothermic hydration reactions. The 
rapid dissolution of 𝐶3𝑆drives the early-age precipitation of crystalline calcium 
hydroxide (Portlandite) and amorphous C-S-H gel, which serves as the primary source 
of compressive capacity. The rapid set times, high early-age strength development, 
and superior quality control of Portland cement quickly displaced traditional lime 
binders, asserting total global dominance across all structural masonry, infrastructure, 
and concrete applications throughout the 20th and 21st centuries. 
To contextualize these historical binders within modern materials science, it is critical 
to evaluate the functional classification and operational boundaries of ancient mortars. 

<<< PAGE 29 >>>
 
12 
 
Ancient architectural engineering possessed no framework for precast structural 
elements or factory-controlled off-site prefabrication. Consequently, the deployment 
of fresh mortars was strictly bifurcated into two functions: joint bedding and protective 
rendering (plastering) [18]. As a joint bedding material, mortar was applied in-situ to 
bind individual unreinforced stone blocks or clay bricks, acting entirely as a plastic, 
compressive load distributor [22]. As a plastering or rendering matrix, it was applied 
as an external surface envelope. This sacrificial barrier protected vulnerable structural 
walls especially sun-dried mud adobe bricks from moisture penetration, wind erosion, 
and physical environmental degradation [23]. Therefore, historical mortar design 
criteria prioritized water retention, surface workability, and environmental sealing 
over high tensile or flexural capacities. The modern demand for rapid factory turnover, 
high early-age demolding strength, and strict control over the aggregate-to-paste 
interface represents an industrial shift. This shift brought intense scientific focus onto 
joint and bond micro-mechanics, establishing the modern engineering requirement to 
chemically modify and mechanically reinforce cementitious composites to meet high 
strength-to-unit density parameters [24]. 
2.2.The Industrial Precast Revolution and the Handling Crisis 
The transition from traditional, in-situ masonry operations to industrialized off-site 
prefabrication represents a significant paradigm shift in civil infrastructure assembly, 
fundamentally redefining the mechanical performance criteria required for 
cementitious binders. For centuries, construction practices relied heavily on the 
sequential, on-site layering of brick and block. This conventional process was heavily 
constrained by environmental dependencies, generated substantial material waste, and 
was subject to the fluctuating quality of manual craftsmanship. The rise of 
prefabrication introduces an automated, factory-controlled molding environment 
where non-load-bearing elements such as partitioning wall panels and facade skins are 
cast under strict, standardized quality controls [25]. 
However, relocating the curing phase from the final building footprint to a remote 
fabrication yard introduces a rigorous set of early-age lifting stress demands. In a 
commercial precast plant, manufacturing velocity directly dictates economic viability. 

<<< PAGE 30 >>>
 
13 
 
Molds must be stripped, and freshly cast panels must be mechanically demolded, 
rotated, lifted, and stacked within a tight 12-to-24-hour cycle. At this early stage, the 
hydration of the Portland cement is incomplete; the matrix has only achieved a fraction 
of its mature tensile capacity and has not yet developed a dense, interlocking network 
of calcium-silicate-hydrate (C-S-H) gels. When high-capacity automated gantry cranes 
pull a green, unripened panel vertically from its mold, the component experiences 
massive localized suction forces and sudden bending moments. These early handling 
forces generate intense internal stress concentrations that can trigger immediate micro-
crack nucleation and drive plastic shrinkage propagation across the thin boundaries of 
the element [25]. Consequently, pre-molded components demand a level of early-age 
structural handling resistance that traditional field mortars were never designed to 
withstand. 
This structural handling risk is directly exacerbated by the high dead-weight penalty 
inherent to traditional dense cementitious composites. Standard cement mortars and 
fine concretes possess an unyielding bulk density ranging between 2200 kg/m³ and 
2400 kg/m³ [18]. When this heavy mass is cast into large-format architectural wall 
panels, the resulting self-weight complicates the entire industrial logistics chain. 
Transporting these massive panels from a centralized manufacturing yard to urban 
high-rise construction hubs necessitates specialized, high-capacity flatbed fleets, strict 
transit permits, and substantial fuel consumption, all of which add to the project's 
overall carbon footprint. Furthermore, once on-site, the installation phase is 
constrained by the strict capacity limits of tower cranes. As a crane hoists a heavy 
panel to the upper levels of a high-rise structure, wind shear and dynamic movement 
expose the dense element to intense kinetic vibrations. If the material lacks sufficient 
tensile capacity and elasticity, these handling forces can lead to brittle fracture 
propagation, structural edge-chipping, or even catastrophic breakage before final 
mechanical anchoring can occur, forcing expensive material replacements and 
significantly delaying the project timeline [26]. 
To overcome these logistical liabilities and engineering bottlenecks, modern materials 
science has introduced the strength-to-unit density ratio as a vital structural parameter 
for optimizing non-load-bearing precast elements [27]. Historically, structural 

<<< PAGE 31 >>>
 
14 
 
concrete design focused almost exclusively on maximizing absolute compressive 
strength (𝑓𝑐). However, for partitioning wall panels and internal separation barriers, 
maximizing absolute compressive capacity is highly inefficient. These elements are 
non-structural and do not carry the primary downward dead-loads of the building 
frame. Instead, their primary structural duty is to safely withstand their own self-
weight, resist localized handling and stripping loads, and endure vertical stacking or 
superimposed dead-loads when multi-panel units are installed sequentially. 
Evaluating mechanical performance relative to volumetric mass shifts the engineering 
focus toward maximizing the specific strength of the material, typically measured in 
MPa/(kg/m³) [28]. Reducing the bulk density by introducing lightweight aggregate 
inclusions lowers the overall dead-load of the panel, effectively minimizing internal 
stress concentrations during lifting and installation operations. However, because 
conventional lightweight aggregate substitutions often cause an exponential drop in 
mechanical performance primarily due to a weakened paste-aggregate boundary 
engineering a viable precast alternative requires precise systematic optimization. The 
material must maintain enough residual strength to survive transportation and vertical 
stacking while simultaneously driving down the overall structural weight. This dual 
engineering demand serves as the foundational motivation for moving past outdated 
trial-and-error mix designs and establishing a structured optimization dataset to 
perfectly balance low density, high strength, and enhanced handling toughness in 
modern lightweight composites.

2.4.Engineering Mechanics of Lightweight Inclusions: The Rationale for EPS 
Integrating ultra-lightweight particulate inclusions within a cementitious matrix 
represents the most direct method for minimizing the dead-weight penalty of precast 
panels, yet its execution introduces severe mechanical trade-offs. Materials literature 
confirms that Expanded Polystyrene (EPS) beads serve as an ideal cellular modifier 
due to their exceptionally low bulk density. While exact figures vary based on 
industrial expansion processes, literature broadly establishes this bulk density as 
ranging widely between 10 kg/m³ and 30 kg/m³ [32]. This extreme lightweight profile 
is driven by a closed-loop microstructure that typically encompasses between 95% and 

<<< PAGE 33 >>>

98% air by volume. Replacing dense aggregates with EPS beads alters the composite 
via physical volume displacement, successfully lowering the final density and 
improving both thermal and acoustic insulation properties. 
However, microstructural studies reveal that raw EPS introduces severe mechanical 
liabilities: compressive and tensile capacities experience an exponential drop as the 
aggregate volume fraction increases. When volumetric replacement thresholds exceed 
ranges of 30% to 50%, studies consistently report that the absolute compressive 
capacity (𝑓𝑐) of unoptimized mortars plummets to critical lows spanning between 1 
MPa and 7 MPa, falling well short of safe structural handling requirements. This rapid 
strength reduction stems from two distinct defects: massive fresh-state aggregate 
segregation and a fundamentally deficient Interfacial Transition Zone (ITZ). Because 
the specific gravity of raw EPS (fluctuating widely in literature between 0.01 and 0.03) 
is a mere fraction of a standard fresh cement paste (which ranges from 1.9 to 2.2), the 
beads face immense upward buoyant forces during casting. During vibration, this 
profound density mismatch causes rapid aggregate flotation, leading to non-
homogeneous material distribution where the weak lightweight polymer localizes at 
the top of the mold while the dense paste settles below. 
            
At the microscopic scale, the core structural weakness of raw EPS is driven by its 
smooth, chemically inert, and hydrophobic surface energy profile. When mixed into a 
fresh cementitious matrix, this hydrophobic boundary repels free mixing water, 
triggering a severe localized packing deficiency known as the geometric "wall effect."

2.5.Chemical Optimization via Micro-Silica: Rice Husk Ash (RHA) Pozzolanic 
Reaction 
 
While physical pre-coating protocols successfully resolve aggregate flotation and 
establish initial surface mechanical interlocking, fully optimizing the bulk matrix 
strength requires targeted chemical refinement of the surrounding cement paste. 
Materials research highlights agricultural waste valorization specifically the extraction 
of Rice Husk Ash (RHA) as an exceptionally effective chemical route for matrix 
densification. Rice husks accumulate in massive quantities during seasonal milling 
operations. When subjected to controlled thermal incineration at temperatures between 
500°C and 700°C, the organic cellulose fractions burn off, leaving behind a highly 
porous residue containing up to 90–95% amorphous silicon dioxide (𝑆𝑖𝑂2) by mass. 
This processed ash exhibits an exceptionally high specific surface area alongside a 
disordered, cellular micro-morphology, classifying it as a highly reactive 
supplementary cementitious material (SCM). Mechanical processing, such as 
optimized planetary ball milling, is critical to reduce the cellular ash particles down to 

<<< PAGE 36 >>>
 
19 
 
a micro-fines size distribution (𝑑50 < 45 µm), thereby satisfying international 
pozzolan standards like ASTM C618 Class N. 
The core chemical advantage of incorporating micro-fine RHA within a lightweight 
mortar matrix lies in its secondary hydraulic pozzolanic reaction, which specifically 
targets the chemical defects inherent in the Interfacial Transition Zone (ITZ). As 
primary Portland cement clinker phases specifically Alite (𝐶3𝑆) and Belite (𝐶2𝑆) 
hydrate, they generate load-bearing calcium-silicate-hydrate (C-S-H) gel alongside a 
substantial, problematic chemical byproduct: crystalline calcium hydroxide 
(𝐶𝑎(𝑂𝐻)2, or portlandite). Portlandite is highly soluble and tends to form weak, plate-
like crystal matrices that orient parallel to aggregate boundaries, effectively acting as 
mechanical failure planes. When reactive RHA is introduced, its highly amorphous 
micro-silica dissolves rapidly into the highly alkaline liquid phase (pH > 12.5) of the 
fresh paste. This dissolution initiates a secondary chemical synthesis that consumes 
the weak portlandite crystals, converting them into stable, secondary C-S-H gel [37]: 
𝐶𝑎(𝑂𝐻)2 + 𝑆𝑖𝑂2 + 𝐻2𝑂→C-S-H 
This chemical transformation precipitates a dense, interconnected mineral gel network 
directly across the localized water pockets and capillary paths generated by the

2.6.Tensile and Toughness Enhancements via Polypropylene (PP) Micro-Fibers 
 
While the physical and chemical modifications targeting the aggregate boundaries and 
the surrounding paste successfully create a dense cement matrix, they also expose a 
fundamental material constraint: structural embrittlement. Materials literature 
demonstrates that as pozzolanic reactions fill internal micro-voids with secondary 
calcium-silicate-hydrate (C-S-H) gels, the resulting hardened paste transitions into a 
highly rigid and brittle state [36]. Under the dynamic handling, demolding, and 
transportation loads unique to off-site precast panel logistics, this rigid composite 
becomes highly susceptible to premature micro-crack nucleation. Because cement 
mortars inherently possess low tensile capacity and low fracture energy, advancing 
crack fronts propagate rapidly through the matrix, causing sudden macroscopic 
shearing or structural breakage. 
Satisfying industrial structural requirements demands upgrading the material's post-
cracking energy absorption. While various fiber types such as steel, glass, and natural 
cellulose are theoretically capable of providing this tensile reinforcement, modern 
materials literature establishes Polypropylene (PP) micro-fibers as the optimal choice 
for lightweight precast mortars due to their exceptional chemical durability and 

<<< PAGE 38 >>>
 
21 
 
specific gravity. Cementitious matrices, particularly those rich in calcium hydroxide 
(𝐶𝑎(𝑂𝐻)2), create a highly alkaline internal pore solution with a pH frequently 
exceeding 12.5. When conventional glass fibers are exposed to this aggressive 
alkalinity, their silica-based structural networks undergo rapid chemical degradation, 
leading to severe long-term embrittlement and strength loss. Similarly, steel fibers 
introduce a substantial dead-weight penalty directly counteracting the fundamental 
lightweight optimization goals of the panel and remain susceptible to moisture-
induced corrosion. 
Conversely, Polypropylene is a synthetic hydrocarbon polymer that is chemically 
inert. Literature confirms that PP micro-fibers exhibit zero chemical degradation or 
loss of tensile capacity, even under prolonged exposure to the extreme alkaline 
environment of hydrating cement [39]. Furthermore, their low specific gravity 
(approximately 0.91) ensures that structural toughness is significantly enhanced 
without adding parasitic weight to the composite.

The underlying mechanism of this mechanical reinforcement relies on a randomly 
distributed, three-dimensional network of fibers that establishes a micro-crack 
bridging web within the mortar. Unlike coarse steel fibers designed to target macro-
scale structural cracking, fine monofilament PP micro-fibers (typically 6 mm in length 
with a 25 µm diameter) introduce millions of individual filaments per cubic meter of 
the mix. When a micro-crack begins to propagate under early-age handling forces, it 
immediately intersects this dense fiber matrix. 
As the crack attempts to widen, the high tensile capacity of the PP fibers absorbs the 
strain energy, transferring the internal tensile stresses back into the surrounding dense 

<<< PAGE 39 >>>
 
22 
 
paste through interfacial friction and mechanical anchoring. This microstructural 
stress-transfer mechanism decelerates crack growth and shifts the failure path from 
sudden brittle rupture to controlled ductile deformation, exponentially increasing the 
material's structural toughness index and post-yield load capacity. 
 
Figure 5 : Mechanical Impact of PP Fiber Dosage Thresholds 
However, incorporating high-aspect-ratio synthetic fibers within a fine-aggregate 
cement mortar introduces critical processing limits, specifically regarding fresh-state 
workability and rheological flow. Research confirms that PP fibers exhibit a high 
specific surface area; when distributed into a wet cement mix, a substantial portion of 
the fresh slurry wraps around the polymer surfaces, reducing the free water available 
to lubricate the fine sand particles. Simultaneously, the physical network mechanics of 
the randomly oriented filaments generate intense internal network friction and shear 
resistance, hindering particle flow and causing measurable drop-offs in slump flow 
values [40]. 
If the fiber dosage exceeds a critical volume fraction, the filaments become entangled 
during mixing, triggering fiber balling and matrix segregation. This clumping behavior 
creates internal mechanical voids and porous paths that paradoxically lower the 
hardened compressive capacity of the mortar. Because high-surface-area pozzolans 
like Rice Husk Ash (RHA) act as internal moisture sinks and PP fibers physically 
restrict flow, a multi-phase optimization approach using Response Surface 
Methodology (RSM) is essential to identify the exact volumetric threshold where 
fiber-bridging efficiency transitions from a fresh-state liability into a mechanical 
advantage [31]. This structural optimization ensures that the ternary lightweight

composite satisfies both the fresh-state flow criteria required for factory casting and 
the post-cracking handling toughness constraints essential for precast logistics. 
2.7.Identifying the Research Gap: Transitioning from Binary to Ternary 
Synergistic Systems 
 
A critical evaluation of existing concrete and civil engineering literature reveals a 
major structural limitation: the vast majority of published studies focus almost entirely 
on binary modifications. Academic repositories contain comprehensive datasets 
mapping the effects of independently adding single modifiers to a plain Portland 
cement mortar. These include assessing the strength losses associated with standard 
Expanded Polystyrene (EPS) inclusions, tracking the chemical pozzolanic reactions of 
independent Rice Husk Ash (RHA) additions, or evaluating the micro-crack arresting 
performance of solitary Polypropylene (PP) fiber meshes. However, analyzing these 
materials as separate, isolated components fails to capture the complex multi-phase 
physical, chemical, and mechanical interactions that govern a ternary matrix. 
In a traditional binary framework, optimizing the composite for a single property 
inevitably triggers a catastrophic performance drop in another. For example, adding 
raw EPS beads achieves a desirable reduction in bulk density but simultaneously ruins 
mechanical capacities due to the aggregate's smooth, hydrophobic boundaries. 
Attempting to rectify this strength deficit by introducing high volumes of porous RHA 
increases the paste density but destroys fresh-state workability. Similarly, introducing 
PP micro-fibers to improve toughness and control shrinkage stresses generates intense 
internal network resistance, further degrading fresh flow characteristics. 
Because these independent components produce nonlinear, conflicting effects in both 
the fresh and hardened states, a traditional one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) experimental 
approach is entirely inadequate for finding a viable engineering balance. Developing 
a true high-performance lightweight mortar for industrial prefabrication requires 
moving past independent binary additions and establishing a multi-phase ternary 
synergy framework.

2.8. Statistical Optimization, Standardization Frameworks, and the Ternary 
Experimental Rationale 
 
The development of structural material composites requires a rigorous validation 
framework that bridges standard testing baselines with advanced statistical design 
models. In concrete materials science, establishing structural reliability begins with 
raw component testing protocols. Local Sri Lankan engineering specifications rely on 
strict standardization baselines, such as SLS 107 for Ordinary Portland Cement 
requirements [41] and SLS 1397 for Manufactured Sand aggregate classification [42], 
which correspond directly to international ASTM and European (EN) standards. These 
frameworks ensure that raw materials satisfy minimum chemical phase stability and 
particle gradation limits before mixing. However, traditional material standards treat 
single components as static variables, offering no guidance when non-standard 
industrial waste streams like Rice Husk Ash (RHA) or compressible inclusions like 
Expanded Polystyrene (EPS) are blended into a multi-phase system. 
To accurately navigate the conflicting performance bounds of a multi-variable matrix 
without resorting to inefficient trial-and-error blending, modern materials research 
relies on Response Surface Methodology (RSM) driven by a Face - Centered Central 
Composite Design (CCF) framework. Traditional one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) testing 
variations isolate a single variable while holding others constant a method that fails to 
capture complex, non-linear chemical and physical interactions. RSM-CCF solves this 
issue by deploying a quadratic mathematical matrix that tracks the interactions of 
multiple factors across a minimum number of distinct runs. For a multi-phase mortar, 
a single CCF matrix generates 11 distinct mix proportions, mapping interactive 
contour gradients to isolate the optimal performance balance between fresh-state flow 
(slump) and hardened capacity curves. 

<<< PAGE 43 >>>
 
26 
 
To manage specific volumetric replacements within this statistical model, mix design 
apportionment relies on the Absolute Volume Method. When low-density inclusions 
like EPS beads are introduced, traditional mass-based batch calculations fail because 
the extreme density mismatch alters the total volume yield of the batch. The Absolute 
Volume Method resolves this by tracking the exact volume occupied by each 
constituent element calculated by dividing its mass by its specific gravity to ensure the 
total combination equals exactly 1.0 m³ of green mortar. This volumetric approach 
separates the mix into two distinct controls: 
• 
The Fixed Matrix Portion: Cement, water, and admixtures remain anchored to 
preserve the baseline matrix integrity. 
• 
The Volumetric Replacement: Dense manufactured sand aggregate is 
systematically substituted by absolute volume fractions of low-density EPS. 
This precise control loop allows for structural substitution while keeping the global 
water-to-binder (𝑤/𝑏) and sand-to-binder (𝑠/𝑏) ratios anchored to the optimal 
baseline center point discovered during plain matrix testing. 
This integrated theoretical framework forms the foundational basis for the five-phase 
research design utilized in this study. Tracking baseline metrics, composite 
optimization, chemical-microstructural validation, physical coating efficiency, and 
fiber-reinforced variations sequentially provides a clear, logical path to address each 
material liability: 
• 
Phase 1 (Plain Baseline Optimization): Uses a CCF matrix to systematically 
vary water and fine aggregate distributions, establishing an optimal baseline 
center point mix to anchor and stabilize all subsequent experimental phases. 
• 
Phase 2 (Composite Matrix Optimization): Employs a secondary CCF matrix to 
evaluate the complex interactive effects of incorporating Coated EPS and micro-
fine RHA. This phase maps the strength and density contours to identify the 
optimal multi-phase mix proportions within the established baseline matrix. 
• 
Phase 3 (Chemical Analytical Validation): Isolates the structural contribution of 
the pozzolanic reaction by maintaining a constant EPS volume and comparing

27 
 
matrices with and without RHA at specific percentages. Microstructural and 
elemental mapping via Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) coupled with 
Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) is utilized to visually identify the 
aggregate-to-paste boundary. Elemental phase allocation and compositional 
gradients are evaluated using EDS atomic line-scans, specifically analyzing the 
element-specific Calcium-to-Silicon (𝐶𝑎/𝑆𝑖) ratio as it directly approaches the 
Interfacial Transition Zone (ITZ) to confirm the formation of dense secondary 
C-S-H gel. 
• 
Phase 4 (Physical Coating Efficiency Validation): Directly compares the 
macroscopic mechanical performance of unoptimized mortars utilizing raw, 
uncoated EPS against those utilizing the engineered PVAc/M-sand coated EPS. 
This phase empirically quantifies the strength recovered by physically 
disrupting the geometric wall effect and preventing aggregate flotation. 
• 
Phase 5 (Mechanical Toughness Upgrade): Systematically varies short 
monofilament polypropylene (PP) micro-fiber dosages within the fully 
optimized matrix to determine the true mathematical optimum. This establishes 
a three-dimensional crack-bridging network to manage the matrix brittleness 
caused by high pozzolanic densification and upgrades the final handling 
toughness of the composite. 
By systematically linking physical aggregate modifications, chemical paste

3.2.2. Design of Experiments (DoE) & Factorial Mix Apportionment 
The multi-variable optimization matrix of this research was configured via Response 
Surface Methodology (RSM) driven by a Face - Centered Central Composite Design 
(CCF) framework. Traditional one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) experimental approaches 
rely on an inefficient trial-and-error methodology that alters a single variable while 
holding others constant. This OFAT approach is fundamentally incapable of detecting 

<<< PAGE 61 >>>
 
44 
 
non-linear interactions between conflicting variables such as the simultaneous 
workability loss caused by combining highly porous Rice Husk Ash (RHA) with high-
aspect-ratio Polypropylene (PP) micro-fibers. RSM circumvents this limitation by 
deploying a quadratic regression matrix that maps interactive contour gradients, 
accurately identifying the synergistic or antagonistic relationships between materials 
while drastically reducing the number of required experimental runs compared to full 
factorial designs. 
The optimization routine was executed across a sequential five-phase timeline. For 
each separate phase involving multiple variables, the CCF framework dictates exactly 
11 distinct mix configurations. This mathematical matrix comprises four factorial 
design points to map linear trends, four axial star points to detect curvature boundaries, 
and three identical center points to isolate experimental error variance and guarantee 
statistical repeatability. Critically, a Face - Centered Central Composite Design (CCF) 
was adopted, setting the axial star point distance to 𝛼= ±1. Extending the axial 
bounds to the traditional rotatable geometry (𝛼= ±1.414) was physically unviable; 
preliminary mixing indicated that exceeding the 𝛼= ±1boundary generated extreme 
factorial limits that yielded entirely unworkable matrices either inducing extreme 
stiffness that prevented compaction, or excessive fluidity that triggered immediate 
lightweight aggregate segregation. 
To maintain strict volumetric accounting across all replacement loops, the mix designs 
were proportioned using the Absolute Volume Method. When introducing ultra-low-
density inclusions like Expanded Polystyrene (EPS) beads, conventional mass-based 
batching fails because the massive density mismatch destroys the volumetric yield of 
the fresh mixture. The Absolute Volume Method ensures that the total combination of 
all constituent volumes equals exactly 1.0 m3of green mortar:

The introduction of ultra-lightweight polymers and highly reactive agricultural

mortar is subjected to a "tug-of-war" between two opposing physical mechanisms: the 
macro-porosity introduced by the Expanded Polystyrene (EPS) boundaries, and the 
micro-filling densification provided by the Rice Husk Ash (RHA).

5.6.1. Flexural Toughness and the Crack-Bridging Mechanism 
The flexural strength (𝑓𝑟) and toughness data clearly validate the necessity of 
discontinuous micro-reinforcement. In the unreinforced baseline (S5M1), advancing 
micro-cracks propagate rapidly through the brittle matrix, yielding a flexural capacity 
of just 3.26 MPa and a baseline toughness of 14.71 mJ/mm³. 
The introduction of PP micro-fibers establishes a randomly distributed, three-
dimensional crack-bridging web. When a micro-crack begins to propagate, it 
immediately intersects this dense fibrous matrix. The high tensile capacity of the PP 
filaments absorbs the strain energy, transferring the internal tensile stresses back into 
the surrounding dense paste through interfacial friction. 
This stress-transfer mechanism is highly effective. As illustrated in Figure 33 (Flexural 
Strength vs. PP Fiber and Toughness vs. PP Fiber), adding fibers creates an immediate 
upward trajectory. At a 0.5% dosage (S5M2), flexural strength increases to 3.59 MPa, 
and handling toughness sees a significant surge to 18.24 mJ/mm³. Flexural capacity 
peaks at 4.00 MPa at the 1.0% dosage (S5M3) before dropping back to 3.65 MPa at 
the extreme 1.5% threshold (S5M4) due to matrix disruption. Macroscopically, this 
fiber integration shifts the failure profile of the panel from sudden, catastrophic brittle 
rupture to controlled, ductile deformation, ensuring the composite can survive the 
dynamic stresses of factory demolding and transit. Beyond this window, however, the 
flexural gains reverse: at 2.0% (S5M5), flexural strength falls to 2.89 MPa below even 
the unreinforced baseline of 3.26 MPa while toughness continues climbing to its 
highest recorded value of 31.54 mJ/mm³. This confirms that past roughly 1.0% fiber 

<<< PAGE 133 >>>
 
116 
 
content, additional reinforcement trades peak load-bearing capacity for pure post-
cracking ductility rather than improving both simultaneously. 
5.6.2. Compressive Capacity and the Fiber Balling Limit 
While flexural capacity scales positively with increased fiber dosage, the compressive 
strength (𝑓𝑐) data reveals a critical structural threshold that dictates the absolute 
optimal mix design. 
• 
The Optimum Threshold (0.5%):  
At the unreinforced baseline, the composite boasts a compressive strength of 
16.78 MPa. Replacing matrix volume with flexible polymer fibers inherently 
reduces this peak density. However, at the 0.5% volume fraction (S5M2), the 
composite safely retains a robust 15.62 MPa. At this low dosage, the fibers 
remain uniformly dispersed. They effectively sew together internal plastic 
shrinkage cracks during the early curing stages without heavily disrupting the 
dense packing of the cementitious paste, thereby preserving the required load-
bearing capacity for precast applications. 
• 
Structural Degradation (1.0% to 1.5%):  
Exceeding the 0.5% threshold triggers a severe mechanical penalty, clearly 
visible as a steep downward trendline in Figure 33. At 1.0% (S5M3), 
compressive strength drops to 14.14 MPa. At 1.5% (S5M4), the capacity 
plummets further to 11.85 MPa, and by 2.0% (S5M5) it declines only slightly 
further to 11.06 MPa indicating the matrix is approaching a degradation plateau 
rather than continuing to collapse at the earlier rate. This rapid degradation is 
driven by the geometric "fiber balling" effect. High concentrations of micro-
fibers drastically reduce fresh-state workability, tangling together to trap 
localized pockets of air during mixing. These entangled fiber masses create 
large, unfillable macroscopic voids within the internal structure. Under 
compressive loading, these voids act as critical stress concentrators, initiating 
premature matrix collapse.

5.6.3. Phase 5 Conclusion (The Final Optimization) 
Phase 5 definitively isolates 0.5% as the optimal volumetric dosage for PP micro-fiber 
reinforcement. This precise fraction maximizes structural efficiency, capturing a 10% 
increase in flexural capacity (from 3.26 to 3.59 MPa) and a 24% increase in handling 
toughness (14.71 to 18.24 mJ/mm³), while successfully preserving a robust peak 
compressive capacity of 15.62 MPa. Dosages exceeding this limit introduce 
catastrophic internal voids that cripple compressive capacity due to fiber balling. 
Testing was extended to 2.0% (S5M5) specifically to confirm this boundary was 
genuine rather than an artifact of a narrow test window: compressive strength 
continued its decline to 11.06 MPa and flexural strength dropped below the 
unreinforced baseline, confirming 0.5% as the true practical optimum. 
With the successful integration of 0.5% PP fibers, the multi-phase engineering 
methodology is complete. The resulting composite utilizing a ternary binder optimized 
with Rice Husk Ash (Phase 2 & 3), physically anchored through PVAc/M-sand 
aggregate coating (Phase 4), and mechanically toughened via micro-fibers (Phase 5) 
satisfies all requisite parameters for the mass production of high-performance, 
lightweight precast partitioning panels.

6. CONCLUSION 
6.1.Summary of the Research 
This comprehensive investigation successfully engineered a high-performance, 
lightweight cementitious composite specifically optimized for the mass production of 
non-load-bearing precast partitioning panels. By systematically addressing the 
inherent structural liabilities of Expanded Polystyrene (EPS) inclusion, the research 
transitioned beyond traditional binary substitutions to establish a highly synergistic 
ternary matrix. Utilizing locally accessible Sri Lankan raw materials specifically Rice 
Husk Ash (RHA) and Manufactured Sand (M-sand) the study mitigated the structural 
dead-weight penalty of precast elements without sacrificing early-age handling 
resilience, fresh-state workability, or long-term mechanical stability. 
6.2.Fulfillment of Primary Objectives 
The multi-phase empirical testing matrix successfully satisfied the two core objectives 
established at the onset of this research, yielding the following definitive conclusions: 
• 
Objective 1: To quantify the independent and synergistic effects of Coated 
EPS, RHA, and PP fiber additions on lightweight mortar performance. 
o Independent Effects Validated: The isolated diagnostic phases 
successfully quantified the individual microstructural and mechanical 
contributions of each material. Phase 3 (Chemical Validation) proved via 
EDS/SEM analysis that RHA independently acts as a chemical welding 
agent, reducing the elemental Ca/Si ratio at the aggregate boundary by 
over 77% and precipitating dense C-S-H gels. Phase 4 (Physical 
Validation) demonstrated that the PVAc/M-sand coating independently 
increases aggregate specific gravity by 1000%, neutralizing the geometric 
"wall effect" and recovering up to 70% of lost compressive capacity. 
Finally, Phase 5 established that a 0.5% monofilament PP fiber addition 
independently increases flexural ductility by 10.1% by establishing a 3D 
crack-bridging web. 

<<< PAGE 136 >>>
 
119 
 
o Synergistic Equilibrium Quantified: The statistical Phase 2 optimization 
successfully mapped the non-linear synergy between these competing 
variables. An absolute ternary equilibrium was isolated at a 10% RHA 
mass replacement and a 20% coated-EPS volume fraction. This specific 
synergistic alignment successfully shed nearly 300 𝑘𝑔/𝑚3of structural 
dead-weight while maintaining a highly stable microstructural void 
architecture (5.79% apparent porosity), definitively proving that chemical 
and physical modifiers operate constructively when volumetrically 
balanced. 
• 
Objective 2: To deliver a generalizable design methodology and validation 
dataset for the lightweight mortar development industry. 
o Methodology and Dataset Delivered: By transitioning away from 
inefficient, traditional trial-and-error blending and deploying Response 
Surface Methodology (RSM) driven by a Face - Centered Central 
Composite Design (CCF), this study successfully established a rigorous, 
predictive mathematical framework. Utilizing exclusively localized Sri 
Lankan raw materials and waste streams, the research generated highly 
significant quadratic regression equations ( 𝑅2 > 0.95 ). These equations 
accurately predict macroscopic compressive strength, flexural capacity, 
apparent porosity, and structural toughness based on precise physical input 
parameters. This dataset directly bridges the empirical validation vacuum 
within the regional precast sector, providing industrial engineers with a 
reliable, generalizable design tool to scale the production of sustainable, 
lightweight partitioning panels. 
6.3.Significant Discoveries (Beyond Initial Objectives) 
Beyond fulfilling the primary mechanical criteria, the progressive testing methodology 
revealed several critical engineering dynamics that push the boundaries of current 
lightweight concrete literature: 

<<< PAGE 137 >>>

• 
Identification of the "Fiber Balling" Threshold: The micro-reinforcement data 
revealed a harsh structural penalty for over-reinforcement. While flexural 
ductility scaled positively up to a 1.5% fiber volume, any dosage exceeding the 
optimal 0.5% threshold triggered severe geometric "fiber balling." This 
physical entanglement trapped macroscopic air voids within the paste, causing 
the compressive capacity to plummet by 24.1% (from 15.62 MPa down to 
11.85 MPa). 
• 
Viability of Extreme High-Volume Substitutions: While 20% EPS represents 
the optimal structural balance, the Phase 4 diagnostics proved that the 
PVAc/M-sand physical coating can successfully rescue extreme 40% 
lightweight volume substitutions from total structural collapse. By forcing 
mechanical interlocking, the coating effectively doubled the flexural capacity 
(a 94.74% increase) of the 40% EPS matrix, proving that ultra-lightweight 
panels are mechanically viable if the aggregate interface is properly 
engineered. 
6.4.Recommendations for Future Research 
While this study establishes a robust microstructural and mechanical foundation for 
ternary lightweight composites, the following areas warrant further investigation to 
facilitate full-scale industrial commercialization: 
1. Long-Term Durability and Permeability Profiling: While the RHA pozzolanic 
synthesis visibly densified the Interfacial Transition Zone (ITZ), empirical 
testing regarding long-term chloride ion penetrability, water sorptivity, and 
sulfate resistance should be conducted to confirm the composite's lifespan in 
harsh environmental conditions. 
2. Thermal and Acoustic Characterization: Given the high volumetric inclusion 
of insulating, cellular EPS and the micro-cellular porosity of the RHA-
modified paste, the resulting composite inherently possesses superior thermal 
insulation and acoustic dampening properties. Quantifying these exact metrics

is essential for marketing the material as an energy-efficient architectural 
solution. 
3. Full-Scale Panel Casting and Dynamic Loading: The optimal mathematical 
mix design (20% Coated EPS, 10% RHA, 0.5% PP Fiber) should be scaled up 
to cast full-dimensional precast wall panels. These scaled units must be 
subjected to dynamic transit simulations and vertical impact testing to 
empirically validate the micro-fiber crack-bridging mechanics under true 
industrial handling stresses.
